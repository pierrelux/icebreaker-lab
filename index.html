What you are looking at

An icebreaker on a voyage through the estuary and Gulf of St. Lawrence. Zoom out and you see the whole voyage; zoom in on the ship and you see its hull breaking the ice. It is one ship and one clock.

The clock follows the zoom. Breaking ice is computed 360 times per second of the ship's time, which a browser manages at about the speed of a real clock. So close to the ship, time runs at 1 to 4 times real speed and the ice is broken for real. Far out, time runs up to two hours per second, and the ship moves at the speed a model fitted to that same ice-breaking computation gives.

The ice is a chart of concentration for one past winter day. The chart gives no thickness, so one thickness is assumed everywhere.

Close to the ship the ice is made from the chart: level ice where the chart shows 90 % or more, floes covering the chart's share of the water below that. The shapes of the floes are invented; the chart only gives a share per kilometre.

The edge of level ice is crushed on the sloping hull, which pushes it down. When the downward force passes the bearing capacity of the ice wedge, P = C_f (θ/π)² σ_f h², a cusp of radius R = C_l ℓ (1 + C_v v_n) snaps off. A floe splits when the in-plane force passes h K_Ic √L.

The other ships are generated: they leave ports at published yearly rates and on winter ferry timetables, keep to their routes, queue in ice, and give way to you nine times in ten. Your ship follows a route the Coast Guard recommended in ice and turns away from ships that come too close. Click the water to send it somewhere else first; A and D steer by hand when you are close.
