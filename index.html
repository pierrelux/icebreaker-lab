What this map shows

An icebreaker on a voyage through the estuary and Gulf of St. Lawrence, on a 2 km grid in steps of one minute. It follows a route the Coast Guard recommended in ice and turns away from ships that come too close. Click the water to send it somewhere else first.

The ice is a chart of concentration for one past winter day. The chart gives no thickness, so one thickness is assumed everywhere.

The other ships are generated: they leave ports at published yearly rates and on winter ferry timetables, keep to their routes, queue in ice, and give way to you nine times in ten.

Your speed is the one at which thrust balances resistance. In ice that resistance is fitted to the ice-breaking simulator of the other view, so the two agree: Break the ice here opens the ice under the ship in that simulator.

How the ice breaks

The hull waterline is checked every half metre against each ice sheet. Where they overlap, the ice edge is crushed at a fixed pressure on the sloping hull face, which pushes the edge down as well as back.

When the downward force passes the bearing capacity of the ice wedge, P = C_f (θ/π)² σ_f h², a cusp of radius R = C_l ℓ (1 + C_v v_n) snaps off and is cut into wedges by radial cracks. ℓ is the characteristic length of a floating plate.

A finite floe splits from the contact point when the in-plane force passes h K_Ic √L, with L the distance the crack must run.

Broken pieces are rigid bodies. Those pushed under the hull add the submersion term of Lindqvist's resistance formula.
